> [!PDF|255,235,77] [[uj_45111+SOURCE1+SOURCE1.1.pdf#page=206&annotation=3090R|uj_45111+SOURCE1+SOURCE1.1, p.206]]
> > The coloniality of power was theorised and introduced by Peruvian sociologist Anibal Quijano (Grosfuguel 2002: 204). The main proposal of Quijano was that modern power is fundamentally constituted by what he terms 'coloniality' (Grosfuguel 2002: 205). The notion of coloniality is not only confined to the domination of the empire over the colonies or the violent rise of the West in relation to the Rest (ibid.). It is not about theorising colonialism in terms of domination alone – both an exercise of power in terms force and consensus as in postcolonial theory - but the overarching and allencompassing racial and capitalist construction of colonial hierarchies which are the exclusionary logic of the modern/colonial regime (ibid., 206).
> 
>

> [!PDF|255,235,77] [[uj_45111+SOURCE1+SOURCE1.1.pdf#page=206&annotation=3097R|uj_45111+SOURCE1+SOURCE1.1, p.206]]

> [!PDF|255,235,77] [[uj_45111+SOURCE1+SOURCE1.1.pdf#page=206&annotation=3103R|uj_45111+SOURCE1+SOURCE1.1, p.206]]
> > Quijano argues that the capitalist colonial modern world system was developed historically by two pillars of exploitation: one is racialisation and other one is capitalist exploitation (ibid.). The coloniality of power is thus primarily the reduction of humanity to the binary classification of human and non-human (Maldonado-Torres 2007: 244). It is a form of classification but also active subjectivisation and reduction of classified human beings.

> [!PDF|255,235,77] [[uj_45111+SOURCE1+SOURCE1.1.pdf#page=206&annotation=3108R|uj_45111+SOURCE1+SOURCE1.1, p.206]]

> [!PDF|255,235,77] [[uj_45111+SOURCE1+SOURCE1.1.pdf#page=207&annotation=3114R|uj_45111+SOURCE1+SOURCE1.1, p.207]]
> > The emergence of colonial relations of power became the history of the world for the last five hundred years. Maldonado-Torres (2007: 243) clarifies: Coloniality is different from colonialism. Colonialism denotes a political and economic relation in which the sovereignty of a nation or a people rests on the power of another nation, which makes such nation an empire. Coloniality, instead, refers to long-standing patterns of power that emerged as a result of colonialism, but that define culture, labor, intersubjective relations, and knowledge production well beyond the strict limits of colonial administrations...It is maintained alive in books, in the criteria for academic performance, in cultural patterns, in common sense, in the self-image of peoples, in aspirations of self, and so many other aspects of our modern experience. In a way, as modern subjects we breathe coloniality all the time and every day. Coloniality is not simply the aftermath or the residual form of any given form of colonial relation.
